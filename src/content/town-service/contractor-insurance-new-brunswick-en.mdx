## How the work runs in New Brunswick

New Brunswick has constant institutional construction: Rutgers, hospitals,
downtown development. Getting onto an institutional project means meeting
insurance requirements written by someone else's legal department, usually with
higher limits and specific clauses your basic policy doesn't carry.

New Brunswick sits in Middlesex County, with roughly 55,266 residents and ZIP
codes 08901, 08903. About 53% of the population is Hispanic, which changes the
conversation: here the paperwork gets done in Spanish or it doesn't get done. A
lot of the people who contact us from New Brunswick also move between Perth
Amboy, Elizabeth and Newark, so what applies here rarely stays here.

Working here means working tight: buildings touching each other, no driveway to
stage materials, neighbors three feet from the scaffold. The risk of damaging
someone else's property is not theoretical, it is daily, and it is exactly what
the property damage side of a liability policy answers. A paint bucket on the
neighbor's car is already a claim.

## What it costs in New Brunswick, in ranges

It is worth walking into this conversation with a range in your head. That way,
when someone gives you a price, you know whether you are in reasonable
territory.

For a small NJ crew, contractor GL policies are commonly published in ranges of
roughly $900 to $3,000 per year. The range depends on trade, years of
experience, the limits you carry and claims history.

That range is wide on purpose, because price moves with concrete factors:

- **The trade.** Roofing, demolition and work at height price differently than painting or finish carpentry.
- **Payroll.** Workers' comp is calculated per $100 of payroll, multiplied by whatever class code rate you are assigned.
- **Whether you use subcontractors.** A sub without their own policy usually ends up counted in your auditable payroll.
- **The property type you work on.** Residential, commercial and institutional carry different requirements and prices.
- **Whether you need endorsements.** Additional insured and waiver of subrogation are policy changes, not just paperwork.

That is what moves the price. What does not move is what the law requires you to
carry, and what the contract requires on top of it.

On the client side, the working standard almost always sits above the state
minimum. A $1,000,000 per-occurrence limit appears in most contracts we see,
along with *additional insured* status. The law does not require that; the
contract does.

The date most often missed is renewal. Every spring, contractors turn up who
discover their registration lapsed, usually because a client went to verify it
before signing.
