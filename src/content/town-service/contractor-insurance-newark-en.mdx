## The real problem for Newark contractors

Newark is the state's largest city, with municipal permits, inspections and
public work. Once you're on a project with city or state money, insurance
requirements become contractual and non-negotiable: fixed limits, certificates
naming the entity, sometimes bonding. It is not the same world as remodeling a
kitchen.

At roughly 311,549 residents and 36% Hispanic, Newark concentrates the kind of
customer nobody explains this to properly, across ZIP codes 07102, 07103, 07104,
07105 in Essex County. A lot of the people who contact us from Newark also move
between Elizabeth, Paterson and Passaic, so what applies here rarely stays here.

Work here spreads across neighborhoods that look nothing alike: single-family
homes in one part, multi-unit buildings in another, storefronts along the main
corridor. Each property type brings its own contract and its own insurance
requirements, and the certificate that worked on one job may not work on the
next.

## What it costs in Newark, in ranges

The numbers below are statewide reference ranges. They are useful for knowing
whether a price you were given is normal, not for predicting yours.

For a small NJ crew, contractor GL policies are commonly published in ranges of
roughly $900 to $3,000 per year. The range depends on trade, years of
experience, the limits you carry and claims history.

That range is wide on purpose, because price moves with concrete factors:

- **The trade.** Roofing, demolition and work at height price differently than painting or finish carpentry.
- **Payroll.** Workers' comp is calculated per $100 of payroll, multiplied by whatever class code rate you are assigned.
- **Whether you use subcontractors.** A sub without their own policy usually ends up counted in your auditable payroll.
- **The property type you work on.** Residential, commercial and institutional carry different requirements and prices.
- **Whether you need endorsements.** Additional insured and waiver of subrogation are policy changes, not just paperwork.

What is not negotiable is the minimum. The state sets one floor and your client
sets another, usually higher.

On the client side, the working standard almost always sits above the state
minimum. A $1,000,000 per-occurrence limit appears in most contracts we see,
along with *additional insured* status. The law does not require that; the
contract does.

What almost nobody checks is whether the policy classification matches the work
actually being done. A policy written for light work does not respond the same
way when the work is structural.
