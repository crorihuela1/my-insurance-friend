## The formula, first

Workers' comp premium is not quoted like auto insurance. It is calculated:

> **(Payroll ÷ 100) × class code rate × experience mod**

Three pieces. Each is worth understanding, because only one of the three is
really in your hands in the short term.

## Piece 1: payroll

This is the total you pay employees over the year. Not revenue, not invoices:
payroll.

Two details that surprise people:

**It is quoted on estimated payroll and corrected against actual.** At the start
of the year you declare what you expect to pay. At the end, the carrier runs an
**audit** and adjusts. Pay more than estimated and a bill arrives. Pay less and
you get a refund or credit.

This is why underestimating payroll saves nothing. It just moves the payment to
a surprise bill months later, when the money is already spent. It is one of the
things that most often wrecks a small contractor's cash flow.

**Subcontractors without their own policy count as your payroll.** This is the
most expensive point in New Jersey. In an audit, a sub who cannot produce their
own workers' comp certificate is added to your auditable payroll, and the
premium is recalculated retroactively to include what you paid them.

The defense is boring and it works: collect each sub's certificate **before**
they start, and keep it.

## Piece 2: the class code

This is the largest factor, and the most misunderstood.

Every type of work has a code, and every code has a rate per $100 of payroll. An
office worker and a roofer on the same wage do not cost remotely the same,
because the odds of falling off a roof are not the same.

This matters more than it sounds. **New Jersey does not use the NCCI**, the
bureau most states use. It uses its own, the NJCRIB. Practical consequences:

- A price you were given in another state does not translate directly.
- NJ class codes are specific to the state.
- Comparing your rate to a friend's in Pennsylvania or New York tells you little.

### Why correct classification matters so much

Two real scenarios:

**Classified too low.** Sounds good until the audit. If you were written as
interior carpentry and you actually do roofing, the audit corrects it and the
difference is charged retroactively. In construction trades that difference can
be several times over, not a percentage.

**Classified too high.** This happens more than people think, especially when a
business does several things and everyone lands under the most expensive code. If
you have someone in the office doing admin, that person usually does not belong
in the same code as the crew on site.

Reviewing classification is one of the conversations that moves the number most,
and it is exactly what a licensed agent is for.

## Piece 3: the experience mod

The **experience modification factor** — the "mod" — is a multiplier based on
your claims history compared to the average for businesses like yours.

- Mod of **1.00** = you are average for your class.
- Mod **below 1.00** = better than average. You pay less.
- Mod **above 1.00** = worse than average. You pay more.

A new business usually starts at 1.00 because there is no history. The mod is
calculated across several years, so a bad year weighs on you for a while — and a
good record also takes time to build.

Something few people know: **frequency weighs more than severity in the mod.**
Several small claims typically raise it more than one large claim. The system's
logic is that many small incidents predict a big one.

With the formula in hand it is clear why nobody serious gives you a number over
the phone: without knowing your trade, payroll and history, any figure is
invented.

What can honestly be said:

- Construction trades sit among the **highest published rates** on the table, far
  above clerical work.
- Within construction, work **at height** — roofing, scaffolding, structural —
  prices above ground-level work.
- For a small crew, workers' comp is usually **the largest insurance expense in
  the business**, well above general liability.

If someone promises you an exact number before asking your trade and payroll,
that tells you something about them.

## A worked example

The numbers below are **illustrative**, not real NJCRIB rates, and exist only to
show how the formula behaves. Real rates are published by the bureau and applied
by the carrier.

Take two businesses, each with **$200,000 of annual payroll**.

**Business A — interior finish work, five years with no claims.**
Hypothetical rate of $6 per $100 of payroll, mod of 0.85.

> ($200,000 ÷ 100) × $6 × 0.85 = **$10,200**

**Business B — roofing, two claims in the last three years.**
Hypothetical rate of $22 per $100 of payroll, mod of 1.25.

> ($200,000 ÷ 100) × $22 × 1.25 = **$55,000**

Same payroll. More than five times the difference.

That is what people mean when they say "construction workers' comp is brutal":
it is not that one carrier is expensive, it is that the trade and the history are
what govern. And it explains why the conversation about **correct
classification** is worth so much: if Business A were mistakenly classified under
Business B's code, it would be overpaying every year without knowing.

It also explains why the mod matters. The gap between 0.85 and 1.25 on identical
payroll and an identical code is nearly 50% of the premium, and that part is
built over time.

## What you can actually change

Of the three pieces, these are the real levers:

**Classification, if it is wrong.** Not a trick: correcting a fact. If you are in
a code that does not describe your work, fixing it changes the number
permanently.

**Claim frequency.** Fewer incidents, lower mod, and the effect lasts years. The
boring things — training, harnesses, a tidy site — are what move the mod.

**Reporting payroll honestly and on time.** It does not lower the premium, but it
eliminates the adjustment bill, which is what actually breaks cash flow.

**Collecting your subs' certificates.** It is the difference between paying for
your people and paying for everyone's.

What is **not** a lever: not carrying the policy.

## What an audit actually looks at

The year-end audit is where the estimate meets reality, and it is worth knowing
what the auditor asks for, because the answer decides your final bill:

- **Payroll records** for the period, by employee.
- **Job descriptions** — what each person actually did, not their title. This is
  what drives classification.
- **Certificates of insurance for every subcontractor** you paid. Missing ones
  get added to your payroll.
- **1099s and cash payments.** Auditors look for these specifically.
- **Overtime records.** In many classifications only the straight-time portion of
  overtime counts toward payroll, which can work in your favor if it is
  documented separately. If it is not broken out, the whole amount may count.

None of this is adversarial by default. An audit with clean records usually ends
in a small adjustment either way. An audit with no records ends with the auditor
making assumptions, and the assumptions are rarely generous.

## What happens without it

In New Jersey, operating without workers' comp while having employees can bring
daily fines and, far more seriously, **personal liability for the owner** for the
injured worker's full medical costs and lost wages. A serious back injury or a
fall can mean hundreds of thousands of dollars in treatment.

Not carrying the policy does not remove the obligation to pay. It moves it
directly onto you and whatever is in your name.

If your crew crosses the state line — normal out of Hudson County — the policy
has to state explicitly which states it covers. A policy issued in NJ does not
necessarily respond to an injury that happens in New York, and NY additionally
requires DBL and Paid Family Leave, which are separate from workers' comp.

Assuming "I'm already covered" is exactly what leaves an injured worker with
nothing and the employer exposed.
