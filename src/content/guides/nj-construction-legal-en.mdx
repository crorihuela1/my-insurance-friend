## Start by separating three things

"What insurance do I need?" is really three questions, and mixing them is why so
many contractors overpay for what they don't need and come up short on what they
do.

1. **What New Jersey requires** for you to register as a contractor and operate
   legally.
2. **What your client requires** — the general contractor, the HOA, the
   commercial building owner — to let you on site. Almost always higher than the
   state minimum.
3. **What you need so an accident doesn't end the business.** Nobody requires
   this. It's the part you decide with an agent.

Meeting the first does not cover the second. Meeting both does not guarantee the
third.

## 1. What the state requires

In New Jersey, nearly anyone doing home improvement work — remodeling, roofing,
plumbing, electrical, flooring, painting, windows — has to register as a **Home
Improvement Contractor (HIC)** with the Division of Consumer Affairs. It is not a
trade license; it is a business registration.

Three details that matter more than they look:

**The minimum is per occurrence, not annual.** $500,000 per occurrence means
$500,000 for each incident. The policy usually also carries an **aggregate** —
the total it pays across the year — and that number is typically double. When a
contract asks for "$500,000/$1,000,000," it means occurrence and aggregate.

**Registration expires March 31, every year.** Not on the anniversary of when you
registered. Every spring we see contractors discover in April that theirs
lapsed, usually because a client checked the state site before signing.

**Since 2025 they also require workers' comp when you have employees**, plus a
compliance bond running $10,000 to $50,000 depending on contract size. That is
new and it caught a lot of people who had renewed unchanged for years.

### Trades with separate licensing

If you are a plumber, electrician or HVAC contractor, HIC registration does not
replace your trade license, which has its own state board and its own insurance
requirements, usually higher. A licensed NJ electrician operates under different
rules than a general contractor.

**From the first employee.** There is no minimum headcount. It does not matter if
they are part-time, temporary, your cousin, or paid in cash.

And here is the most expensive mistake in this state: **calling someone a
"subcontractor" does not make them one.** In an audit, what decides is not the
label or the payment method, but the actual working relationship:

- Who directs the task and decides how it gets done?
- Who supplies the tools and materials?
- Does that person carry their own workers' comp policy?
- Do they work for other clients, or only for you?

If the answers point to you directing and you supplying everything, that person
counts as your employee and their pay is added to your auditable payroll. The
premium is recalculated retroactively and the difference arrives as an
adjustment bill.

This is why the healthy habit is to collect each subcontractor's **workers' comp
certificate** before they start, and keep it. Without that paper, in an audit
that sub is your employee.

### What happens without it

In New Jersey, operating without workers' comp while having employees can bring
daily fines and — far more seriously — **personal liability for the owner** for
the injured worker's full medical costs and lost wages. Not having the policy
does not remove the obligation to pay. It moves it onto you.

## 3. What your client requires

This is where the state minimum stops mattering.

The working standard in most contracts we see across Bergen, Passaic, Essex and
Morris County is **$1,000,000 per occurrence / $2,000,000 aggregate**. The law
does not require that. The contract does, and for that job the contract governs.

Beyond the limit, contracts usually ask for things that are **not part of your
policy by default** and have to be added as endorsements:

**Additional insured.** The general wants to be named on your policy, so that if
someone sues them over something you did, your insurance responds. It is an
endorsement, not a piece of paper: you have to request it from the carrier and
it sometimes costs.

**Waiver of subrogation.** You give up your carrier's right to pursue the general
to recover what it paid. Common on commercial work.

**Primary and non-contributory.** Your policy pays first, before the general's,
without sharing.

**Certificate holder.** Who receives the certificate. This one really is just a
field on the paper.

The practical difference: a certificate comes out same day because it only
reflects what you already have. **An endorsement is a change to the policy and
can take days.** If the contract asks for additional insured and you request the
COI Thursday for a Monday start, that is where the job falls apart.

## 4. What the law doesn't require but can save you

These coverages appear in no state requirement. They exist because the risks do.

**Tools and equipment.** General liability **does not cover your tools.** GL
responds to damage you cause a third party. If your truck gets emptied, that is a
different coverage, which the industry calls *tools and equipment* or *inland
marine*, and it is not included by default.

**Commercial auto.** If the vehicle is titled to the business, or you use it to
haul materials and tools to job sites, a personal policy can deny the claim for
commercial use. It is one of the most common and most expensive gaps.

**Installation floater.** Covers material you have bought and installed but not
yet handed over. If the windows you set yesterday get stolen, that is not a GL
claim.

## The five mistakes we see constantly

**1. Quoted as "handyman," working as structural.** Your policy's classification
describes the work you do. If it was written for light work and you are taking
out load-bearing walls or touching foundations, you have a gap: the carrier can
dispute a claim on the basis that the real risk was never the one it priced. When
the kind of work you take changes, say so.

**2. Letting the policy lapse for a month.** A coverage gap does not just leave
you exposed for those days. When you re-insure, the history of a gap puts you in
a higher price tier, and some carriers simply will not quote someone cancelled
for non-payment.

**3. Assuming a COI can be "sorted out" same day.** The certificate can. The
additional insured endorsement, not always. When you sign a contract, read the
insurance section **that day**, not the week work starts.

**4. Not keeping subcontractor certificates.** At the year-end audit, every sub
without proof of their own coverage is added to your payroll. We have seen
five-figure adjustment bills that a folder of PDFs would have prevented.

**5. Reporting an "estimated" payroll that doesn't resemble the real one.**
Workers' comp premium is calculated on projected payroll and trued up against
actual at close. Underestimating does not save money: it just moves the payment
to a surprise bill months later, when it has already been spent.

## So what does it cost?

Published ranges for a small New Jersey crew run roughly **$900 to $3,000 a
year** for general liability. Workers' comp has no comparable range because it is
not quoted off a list price: it is calculated per $100 of payroll, multiplied by
the NJCRIB class code rate and your experience mod.

That last part explains why two contractors with identical payroll pay very
differently. A roofer and a painter with the same total wages do not cost the
same, because the fall risk is not the same. And a business with five clean years
carries a lower mod than one with two recent injuries.

These are reference ranges, not a quote. They are useful for knowing whether a
price you were given is normal.

## How it fits together

For a small NJ crew doing residential work and occasional commercial, the typical
structure is:

- **Current HIC registration**, renewed before March 31.
- **CGL** of at least $500,000 per occurrence for the state, almost always
  $1,000,000 in practice because contracts ask for it.
- **Workers' comp** from the first employee, with certificates kept for every
  subcontractor.
- **Compliance bond** sized to your contracts.
- **Tools, commercial auto** and the rest, depending on how you work.
