## What a COI is, in one sentence

A **Certificate of Insurance** is a one-page summary of a policy that already
exists. It is not the policy. It is not coverage. It is proof that says: this
business carries this insurance, with these limits, in force between these dates.

That explains the most important thing in this guide:

> **If your policy is already active, the certificate comes out the same day.**
> Usually within the hour, because there is nothing to decide. It just prints
> what is already true.

It also explains the reverse: with no policy, no COI is going to appear, however
urgent it is. There is no certificate without the coverage behind it.

## What the document looks like

The industry standard form is the **ACORD 25**. If someone sends you something
that does not look like an ACORD 25, it is worth asking why. The sections people
actually check:

- **Insured.** Your business, with the exact legal name. If your LLC is "JR
  Construction LLC" and the certificate says "JR Construction Co.", it gets
  rejected.
- **Insurer.** The carrier that issued the policy.
- **Coverages.** Which policies you hold: general liability, auto, workers' comp,
  umbrella. Each with its policy number and effective dates.
- **Limits.** The numbers. This is where the general looks first.
- **Certificate holder.** Who receives the certificate.
- **Description of operations.** The box where additional insured status, waiver
  of subrogation and sometimes the job address appear.

## How to request one, step by step

**1. Get the requirement in writing.** Not verbally. Ask for the contract
paragraph or the email where the general says exactly what they need. What
changes the job is not "send me your insurance" — it is the detail: what limit,
naming whom, with which endorsements.

**2. Forward it to your agent as-is.** Do not summarize. Half the problems come
from a contractor saying "they want a million" when the contract also asked for
additional insured and primary and non-contributory.

**3. Give the exact legal name and address of the certificate holder.** As it
appears in the contract, LLC or Inc included. A misspelled name is the number one
reason a certificate gets sent back.

**4. Say which job it is for** and when you start. If the work has a specific
address, it usually goes in the description box.

**5. Check it before you send it.** Effective dates covering the whole project,
limits meeting or beating what was asked, your name spelled right.

## What comes same day and what doesn't

This is the distinction that decides whether you start Monday.

**Same day:** the certificate itself. It is generated from the existing policy.
Many agencies issue it in minutes and email it.

**Can take longer:** anything that is an **endorsement** — a change to the policy:

- **Additional insured.** Adding the general as an additional insured. Some
  policies carry a "blanket" endorsement covering anyone you have a written
  contract with; if you have that, it is immediate. If not, it has to be
  requested from the carrier and can take a day to a week, sometimes at a cost.
- **Waiver of subrogation.** Same thing: a change to the policy.
- **Raising the limit.** If you carry $500,000 and the contract wants
  $1,000,000, that is not a different certificate. It is a different policy, or
  an umbrella on top.

**So the practical rule is:** read the insurance section the day you sign the
contract, not the week work starts. If there are endorsements to request, that
week of lead time is exactly the margin you need.

## The "blanket additional insured" question worth asking

Many commercial contractor policies include an endorsement called *blanket
additional insured*, which automatically adds any person or company you are
required by written contract to name.

If your policy has it, requesting additional insured stops being a multi-day
errand and becomes immediate: the agent simply reflects it on the certificate.

If it does not, every new general is a separate request.

It is one of the most useful questions you can ask your agent, and almost nobody
asks it.

On a certificate, workers' comp appears on its own line. Two common situations:

**You have employees.** The certificate shows the workers' comp policy with its
employer's liability limits.

**You work alone.** Many generals accept an **exemption certificate** instead of
a policy, but not all: on some sites the general's or owner's own policy requires
coverage with no exceptions, even where the law does not.

## Mistakes that get a certificate bounced

- **Insured name different from the contract.** You signed as "JR Construction
  LLC" but the policy is in your personal name.
- **Dates that don't cover the whole project.** The policy expires mid-job. The
  general notices.
- **Missing the additional insured** the contract asked for, because nobody read
  it.
- **Limits below what was requested.**
- **Certificate holder misspelled** or the wrong address.
- **Sending last year's certificate.** It happens more than you would think.

## What a COI does NOT prove

This gets misunderstood in both directions, and it is worth being clear whether
you are requesting one or handing one over.

**A certificate is not coverage.** It is a snapshot of the policy on the day it
was issued. If the policy is cancelled the following week, the certificate still
sits on the general's computer, but there is no insurance behind it. That is why
serious generals ask for current certificates, not one from eight months ago.

**A certificate does not modify the policy.** The description box saying
"additional insured" does not create the coverage on its own: the endorsement has
to exist on the actual policy. A certificate referencing an endorsement that does
not exist is a serious problem, and it is the kind that surfaces exactly when
there is a claim.

**A certificate does not show exclusions.** A contractor policy can exclude work
at height, demolition, roofing or water damage, and none of that appears on the
ACORD 25. The certificate shows limits, not exclusions.

If you are the one hiring a subcontractor, that means the certificate is the
floor of your verification, not the ceiling.

## If the client is a homeowner, not a general

Working direct for a homeowner, you are rarely asked for a COI formally, but more
and more people ask "are you insured?" before signing. In the Morris, Bergen and
Somerset suburbs that question arrives before the one about price.

Showing a clean certificate at that moment does two things: it answers the
question, and it separates you from whoever cannot answer it. Several contractors
we have spoken to send it along with the estimate, unasked, for exactly that
reason.

## Three quick questions

**Can I issue the certificate myself?** No. It is issued by the agency or carrier
holding the policy. A certificate produced by the contractor has no value, and
editing one is fraud.

**Does it cost money?** The certificate usually does not. Endorsements sometimes
do, depending on the carrier and what is being asked for.

**How many can I request?** As many as you need. Having a different certificate
for each general is normal, because each one is issued in their name.

## What to do if you need one today and have no policy

Be straight with the general about the real timeline. A new commercial policy can
be issued quickly — sometimes same day with some carriers — but it needs business
information: estimated revenue, payroll, years operating, type of work, claims
history.

What does not work is promising a certificate for tomorrow with nothing behind
it. If the general finds out the paper does not exist, you lose the job and the
relationship.

## Keep everything

A boring piece of advice that saves weeks: keep a folder on your phone with your
current HIC registration, your most recent COI, and workers' comp certificates
for every subcontractor. Most jobs that fall through over paperwork fall through
because the paperwork existed but was not to hand the day it was asked for.
